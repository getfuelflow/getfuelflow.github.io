Nutrition for Strength Training

Lifting builds strength, power, and resilience for almost every sport. But the gym only does half the work. Your muscles get stronger while you recover, and food, sleep, and consistency are what make that happen.

Before you lift

Training on an empty stomach usually means weaker lifts and less focus. Have a meal 2 to 3 hours before, or a small snack 30 to 60 minutes before:

  • A banana with peanut butter
  • Toast or a bagel with honey
  • Yogurt with fruit
  • A granola bar and a glass of milk

After you lift

Within about 1 to 2 hours, eat a meal or snack with both protein and carbohydrates. Protein helps repair and build muscle, and carbs refill the energy your muscles used.

  • Chicken or beef with rice and vegetables
  • Eggs with toast and fruit
  • Greek yogurt with granola and berries
  • Chocolate milk and a sandwich

Spread protein through the day

Your body uses protein best when it's spread out, not crammed into one big meal. Aim for a source of protein at every meal and most snacks: eggs at breakfast, chicken or beans at lunch, fish or meat at dinner, and yogurt or milk as snacks. For more detail, see How Much Protein Do Athletes Need?

Don't cut the carbs

A lot of gym advice focuses only on protein, but carbohydrates fuel hard training. Without enough, workouts feel harder and recovery is slower. Rice, pasta, bread, potatoes, oats, and fruit all belong in an athlete's diet.

Sleep is part of nutrition

Most muscle repair happens while you sleep. Teens generally need 8 to 10 hours a night. No food or supplement makes up for consistently short sleep.

About supplements

  • Most athletes can get all the protein they need from regular food.
  • Protein powder can be convenient, but it isn't required to build muscle.
  • Teens should talk to a doctor before taking any sports supplement, including protein powders, pre-workouts, and creatine. Supplements aren't tested as strictly as medicine, and some contain ingredients that aren't on the label.
